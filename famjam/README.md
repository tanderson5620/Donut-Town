# FAM JAM: Andersons vs Gormans vs Stowells vs Joseph George vs Flickinger (2D arcade)

A 2-on-2 arcade basketball game in the style of the classic 90s arcade cabinets: side-on camera that slides along the court, a packed crowd, digitized-looking players with big heads, SHOOT / PASS / TURBO, monster dunks and "on fire" streaks. Five teams: the Andersons, the Gormans, the Stowells, Joseph George and Flickinger, two of them in each game. Made for phones (landscape) and desktop browsers. Plain HTML + JavaScript on a 2D canvas, no build step and no 3D at runtime.

Play: https://tanderson5620.github.io/Donut-Town/famjam/ (the old `/jam/` link redirects here). On iPhone, Share > Add to Home Screen runs it fullscreen.

Promo poster: `img/poster.png` (1200 x 1600; Zach powered up over Ramsey, Jahn and Sque twerking, drawn by the game's own `X.drawPlayer` over its arena, with the logo and the families' emblems from the menus, `UI.emblem`, `UI.flaming`, `UI.bigBall`).

## Controls

| | Phone | Keyboard | PlayStation controller |
|---|---|---|---|
| Move | Left thumb (joystick appears where you touch) | Arrows / WASD | Left stick or D-pad |
| Shoot | Hold SHOOT, release at the top of the jump (green on the meter) | Hold J or Space | Hold CROSS |
| Turbo | Push the stick past its ring (the ring is your turbo meter and lights up) | Shift or L | R2 (or R1, L2, L1) |
| Dunk | DUNK button (lit when you're in range; pressed up to 0.45 s early it still goes as soon as you're there), or turbo + SHOOT running at the rim | Shift/L + J | CIRCLE, or R2 + CROSS |
| Pass | PASS | K | SQUARE |
| Shove | SHOVE button (the third button whenever you don't have the ball, offense or defense) | Shift/L + K | CIRCLE, or R2 + SQUARE |
| Defense | SHOOT = jump/block, PASS = steal | same keys | CROSS = jump/block, SQUARE = steal |
| Teamwork | PASS with no ball = call for it; SHOOT = tell your teammate to shoot | same keys | same buttons |
| Pause | II | Esc / P | OPTIONS |

**Controllers** (`js/pad.js`, the browser's Gamepad API): a PlayStation controller (DualSense, DualShock 4) - or any controller the browser gives the standard layout - over USB or Bluetooth on a computer, phone or tablet. On the menus it gets a cursor, a thick blinking frame round a button: the D-pad or stick moves it to the nearest button that way, CROSS presses it (with nothing picked, the screen's own Enter), CIRCLE goes back; it starts on the screen's main button (PLAY, RESUME, a pop-up's JOIN). The title shows the controller's buttons with the PlayStation symbols, drawn (`P.glyph`), a note says PLAYSTATION CONTROLLER CONNECTED, the thumb buttons hide on a phone while it's in use, and the big hits rumble it (they follow the screen shake, `dual-rumble`). A headset's own controllers on the VR page aren't standard-layout gamepads, so they're left to vr.js. (A game on the PlayStation itself, from its store, would need Sony's developer program.)

**On the PlayStation itself, in its web browser** (`js/ps.js`; PS4: the Internet Browser app, type the address; PS5: it has no browser app; the usual way in is a link in a message - send the game's link in a PlayStation App message, to a friend or a group you're in, then select it in Game Base on the console and it opens in the built-in browser). Those browsers may keep the controller for themselves (the left stick moves the browser's pointer, CROSS clicks, CIRCLE goes back a page) and pass only some buttons to the page as key presses, so the game takes whatever the console gives it (it knows from the browser's user agent):
- the controller through the Gamepad API (`pad.js`), when the browser hands it over - then everything above works as is;
- **cursor play** otherwise: your player runs to the pointer (a yellow ring on the floor where he's headed; the pointer far off, 4.5 m+, is turbo and the ring goes orange), and CROSS - a click - does what fits where it points: on your teammate it passes (or calls for it), on the man with the ball it steals, on another opponent it shoves, on your rim it dunks, anywhere else it shoots (hold, let go at the top) or jumps to block. CIRCLE, the browser's back, pauses (or goes back a menu) instead of leaving the game (an extra page kept in the history, `popstate`). The thumb buttons stay hidden (the pointer isn't fingers), the menus work by pointing and clicking, Enter shoots;
- **SET UP BUTTONS** (top left of the title screen, only on a PlayStation): press the button you want for SHOOT, PASS / STEAL, SHOVE / DUNK, TURBO and PAUSE in turn and whatever the browser sends for it is learned and kept on that console (`famjam.pskeys`; key numbers too, for browsers that give nothing else - `P.code`, used by the keyboard for everyone); a button that sends nothing is one the browser keeps, so SKIP it. The same screen shows what the controller last sent and whether a gamepad is seen, to find out what that console passes on.
Not tried on a real console (no PlayStation to test on): it's built from what's known about those browsers and tested in a browser told it's a PS5 (`?ps` does the same on any computer). Sony's browsers may not do WebRTC, which online play and voice chat need, so on a PlayStation it may be play-the-CPU only.

Ratings (1-10): SPD, POWER, 3PTS, STEAL, DUNK, BLOCK and PASS. POWER wins shoves, dunk rejections (a stronger blocker flattens the dunker for 2 s), posterizes and box-outs. PASS makes passes faster, truer and harder to intercept (`safe` in `launchPass`: a PASS 10 pass is picked off about a third as often as a PASS 3 one), and they home in on the receiver (`home`, heat-seeking): on the way the ball bends toward where he'll be when it gets there, following all of his drift at PASS 9+ (or on fire) - so it finds him however he runs or cuts, and only an interception stops it - about half of it at PASS 5 and a quarter at PASS 3 (the aim swings at most 30 m/s, so a cut back gets a curve, not a jump; the computer's receivers meet it where it was thrown, `pass.ax`/`az`). Against a receiver who keeps running at full speed, no defenders, a PASS 10 pass now always arrives (it used to miss every time he ran across), PASS 5-6 gets there going away or toward the passer and mostly running across, PASS 3 about half the time. And good passers show off: behind the back (wrapping round his back), a 360 spin, a flip with the release at the top, a football snap back through his legs to a teammate behind him, over the head, a no-look, or a moonwalk (gliding backward toward his man, then a flick back over the shoulder) - the ball stays in his hand through the move.

Four quarters of 1:00 (default), 1:30, 2:00 or 3:00, picked on the team card and remembered on the device; overtime is half a quarter (30-60 s).

After a basket (or a shot-clock violation) nobody is teleported back into place: the other team takes it out under the basket it was just scored on and passes it in, like real basketball (`G.startInbound`, phase 'inbound', `G.inb`). The player nearest the baseline takes it out - a computer player when there is one, so you're the one bringing it up - walks out behind the baseline with it and throws it in to his partner; you can press PASS to call for it on his way out, and it comes the moment he's out on the baseline - never before (`ib.ask`; PASS used to throw it in from wherever he was). A person taking it out throws it with PASS once he's there, or it goes on its own after 2.5 s. The clocks wait for the pass, the inbounder can't be stripped, shoved or arm-barred out there, and an inbound pass is easy to catch (2.8 m reach). Meanwhile the team that scored has to get back on D. Each team has a safety, the slower of its two players (`G.safetyOf`, by real speed): with his partner on the ball he trails the play at the top of the arc instead of going deep, and the moment a shot of his team's goes up - a jumper, a layup, a dunk - he heads back to the top of the key at his own end (`G.backMan`; if he's the one shooting, his partner goes instead), so the slow man is already back to stop the fast break. When it's you, nothing on screen tells you - getting back is up to you.

Three straight baskets by one player sets him on fire (flaming ball, unlimited turbo, near-automatic shots) until the other team scores.

The final scoreboard plays the game's highlights behind the stats (`js/replay.js`) instead of a plain blue card. On fire, his best 3 ratings jump to 10 for as long as he stays on fire. On top of that, his best rating (his specialty) is turned up even more: speed - 30% faster with afterimages; pass - every pass is a fancy move and can't be intercepted; 3PTS - every release is green, about 99% from anywhere, and the net catches fire on a three; dunk - takes off from much farther out, longer hang time in flames, usually shatters the glass, and only a defender on fire can reject it; block - blocks almost everything with longer reach; power - every shove flattens and nobody can move him; steal - much better steals and interceptions.

3-point shooters (Tyler, Karen, Rox, Morgan, Sque) pulling up for a three while moving side to side (across the line to the rim) do a Harden step-back first (`stepback`: gather the dribble, hop back about 0.7 m, land square, then the jump shot 0.34 s in; let go of SHOOT during it and the shot goes up on its own at the top of the jump, and now and then a make is called STEP-BACK THREE!). The step-back makes space: from the press until the ball's gone nobody can steal it or shove him (`G.guarded`), and the shot can't be blocked (`shotInfo.sure`), while a hand in his face counts only half the usual contest (`contest`: it used to count for nothing) - with a defender right on him a well-timed step-back goes in about 8 times in 10, against roughly 5-6 in 10 for a plain three. The green window is always timed from the press of SHOOT (`p.sht`, `G.shotApex`): the sweet spot is when the top of a plain jump shot would come, whether he steps back or cartwheels first, so the same hold gives the same result every time; the shot meter over YOU starts filling on the press, during the move too, and shows the real zones the release is judged by (`G.shotWin`): green in the middle, amber (ok) either side out to 2.5 times as wide, dark red (bad) beyond - the green half again wider (x1.6) for a 3-point shooter on a three. Anywhere inside a zone counts the same: green adds a flat bonus, ok adds nothing, bad cuts the chance by almost half. A let-go during the move is judged right then (`p.relPress`), the ball going up at the top of the jump. Online a guest's let-go is timed on his own phone, press to release (`pressAt` in net.js), and his meter runs off the same press. They shoot a Kobe-style fadeaway when they pull up while moving away from the basket (`shoot_fade`: leaning back, the near knee kicked out, drifting away from the rim), and a LaMelo-style one-foot three when they pull up running at it (`shoot_onefoot`: off the far foot, the near knee driving up and forward and staying up through the shot, carried a little toward the rim; the same odds as any three, from where he took off, `p.shotFrom`; now and then a make is called ONE-FOOT THREE!), and about 2 in 10 of their made threes end in a Steph-style shoulder shimmy once they land (`shimmy`). Now and then (`CART_P`, 1 in 20 of their other threes - standing or moving toward the rim) a 3-point shooter cartwheels first: straight on to the camera he wheels about 1.7 m away from the rim in 0.8 s (`cartwheel`, the body turning round his hips, `wheel` in gen.html), one-handed with the ball tucked at his hip, lands turned back toward the rim and chucks it one-handed (`shoot_chuck`, ball cocked behind his head). It runs off his anim and frame, so online guests see it too), a front flip, a mid-air double-biceps flex to the camera, a bounce to himself, and from way out the stretched-arm cartoon dunk or the free-throw-line dunk (tongue out). The Max Strength players (Lisa, Kody, Uncle Kenny, Blair, Justin, Ramsey, Austin, and The Legend, below) now and then turn a shove into an arm bar - rare: 1 in 20 of the shoves that reach a man, about 1 in 30 of all their shoves counting the ones that hit nobody (after a photo of an MMA arm bar; `G.startArmbar`, `ARMBAR_P`): a 2.5 s close-up of the two of them on the floor (`G.cine` kind 'armbar', `updateArmbarCine`) - he grabs the arm, takes him down, both legs across him, pulls the wrist into his chest and bridges: SNAP!, then gets back up - while the rest of the game waits: nobody else moves, the ball hangs where it is and the game clock and shot clock stop, so it can't cost either team anything; play picks up again the moment he's up. His man is down for 10 s (`p.hurtT`, a red-cross badge counting it down over him) and stays down through every stoppage: baskets (his team scoring too: he doesn't jump up to cheer), restarts and quarter breaks leave him where he lies (`G.lineup` skips him), nobody can pass to him and he contests nothing; then he gets up and plays on. New anims `armbar` (the strength players) and `armbarred` (everyone) in the extras sheets.

The 3-point shooters' low blow (`tryKick`, `KICK_P`): going up for a jump shot with a man close enough to contest it in front of them (within 1.6 m), 1 in 50 of those shots their near leg snaps out straight just off the floor and lands in his crotch (`shoot_kick`). The shot always goes in and can't be stripped or blocked (`p.kick`, `shotInfo.sure`); his man doubles over, drops to his knees and rolls onto his back clutching it, rocking (`nutted`), down for 5 s (`KICK_HURT`, the same countdown badge and stays-down rules as the arm bar), LOW BLOW!, a punch and a groan (help for the guys, a grunt for the women). The game doesn't stop: 1 s of slow motion from the kick (`G.cine` kind 'kick', play at 0.4x, so 0.4 s of play), the camera in close on the two of them, then on at full speed. The 3-point shooters shoot like Steph Curry (`shoot_steph`, their own jump shot in the extras sheets): a quick one-motion release - the ball low at the hip on the gather, lifted in one smooth motion past the face to a set point just above the forehead (the game carries the ball between his palms, `meta.hand`) and let go early, on the way up, the guide hand peeling off, then a high follow-through. Their sweet spot is there too (`G.relFrac`, `QUICK` 0.45 of the way to the top of the jump; everyone else's is the top), so on the shot meter their green starts right by the start of the bar - on a three about 0.04-0.29 s after the press, the first half of the bar - and the computer's shooters let it go there, before a man closing out can get a hand up. The fadeaway, one-footer, kick and cartwheel chuck keep their own frames, on the same early timing. And the quick release gets it off before anyone can close the space (`QR_NEAR`, `QR_U`, `shotInfo.near`): only a man right in his face - within 0.8 m, chest to chest - as it leaves his hands gets a try at blocking it, and only right then (the first 20% of its flight); anyone else can't get there in time to try. (Every other jump shot can still be blocked by anyone in the air near the ball for the first half of its flight: 45% of it for the computer, 60% for you.)

Three or two goes by where the shooter left the floor (`takeoff`: `p.shotFrom`, or where a cartwheel started), not where the ball leaves his hand. You can drift in the air, so rising from behind the line and letting go inside it is still a three, and a fadeaway that starts inside the line and drifts out is a two - the same rule as the 3-point dunk. (It used to go by the release spot, so both of those came out wrong.)

The 3-point shooters get 20% more turbo than everyone but the Ball Handlers: their meter drains at 1/1.2 the rate (`turboDrain` 0.833 in `HW.TYPES`), so a full one lasts about 4 s instead of 3.3 s (the handlers' lasts 5.6 s).

The stats screen at the end of a game shows the level it was played on, top right (EASY LEVEL in green, NORMAL in yellow, HARD in red).

Range on threes: a shot from just behind the arc keeps its full odds; from farther out they fall off fast (`deepF` in `G.releaseShot`) - a great shooter's perfectly timed three, about 89% open from the line (TP 10; 0.11 + 0.05 a point of 3PTS before the green bonus, `G.releaseShot`), is about 58% from 2 m behind the arc, 19% from half court and 1% from his own end (it used to be the same from anywhere, so backpedaling for full-court threes was a cheap win). On fire buys a little more range, not all of it.

The chase-down block (after LeBron's, `chaseDown` in game.js): when a Dunker (Carl, Kameron, Trent, Jake, Cesar) rejects a Ball Handler's layup or dunk, he comes flying in from behind and pins it flat on the glass (`block_chase`, 5 frames in the dunkers' extras sheets: the near knee driving up, the far leg trailing, the near hand open and stretched up to the board, his arm drawn over the photo head) - 1 s of slow motion in close on the two of them (`G.cine` kind 'chase', play at 0.35x, the same as the low blow) with BLOCKED BY JAMES! across the bottom of the screen (a `low` callout, clear of the close-up); he comes down with the ball and the point guard drops back to the floor empty-handed.

The Ball Handlers (Gator, Bianca, Katelyn, Lindsay, Zach, Jahn - the speed players) mix layups in with their dunks: half their drives are a layup, with the same glide, hang time and block window as a dunk (`G.rejectDunk`), the ball leaving the hand right where a dunk would slam and then flying in (`shotInfo.lay`, never blockable after the release, just like a dunk after the slam). A layup always uses the outside hand for the side of the rim he's on: on the near half of the court (z below the rim's, nearer you) his near hand, on the far half his far hand. ## Balance (simulated)

The game rules run headless at about 4 games a second, so balance gets checked by simulating thousands of all-CPU games (random pairs from two different families, Normal, 1:00 quarters). From 10,000 games: the families came out close (Andersons 54%, Stowells 49%, Gormans 48% of games won), but the team that moved first each frame won only 45.6%: whoever moves second sees where the others just went. The two teams now take turns moving first, frame by frame (`G.flip` in `G.update`), and it's 50.1% over 3,000 games.

The computer's 3-point shooters were the weakest players by far (32% of games won, against 58% for the ball handlers): they fired from 9 m, stood still with the ball and timed green only 35% of the time. Its play now: shooters spot up just behind the arc, shoot on the catch and slide along the arc when covered; nobody stands still with the ball; jump-shot timing goes by the difficulty (40 / 60 / 80% green); its steals land a quarter less often. Over 10,000 games on Normal: standing still with the ball went from 2-4 s a game per player to 0-2 s, the computer's shooters hit 62% of their threes (43% before), handlers average 4 steals (5.5 before), scoring went from 89 to 98 points a game - and the shooters win 33.5% (32% before), the handlers 58.6%. What's left for shooters is defense: their teams still give up about 3 points a game more than average.

The real inbound and the safety, over 10,000 games against the old reset (everyone back at half court, the ball to the speed player with the defense spread out in front of him): after a basket the man bringing it up scores on his own 34% of the time instead of 53%, the speed player brings it up 24% of the time instead of 52%, blowouts (20+) drop from 16% to 8% and close games (5 or less) rise from 31% to 36%. Ball handlers win 55.6% (58.6% before), two speed players together 54% (60%), two max-strength players 53% (45%); dunkers 58.5%, shooters 33.5%.

After the quick release, the threes at the line and the extra turbo, the shooters had swung to the top: over 6,000 games they won 61%, the handlers and dunkers 50% and the max-strength players 44%, and in a 30,000-game matrix (100 games of every pairing) Carl + Tyler won 73%. The computer's bigs settled for close jumpers at the rim, and the shooters' step-back went in 81% of the time with nothing able to touch it. Three changes, together, over 3,000 games: the other three types finish at the rim (above), the step-back counts half a contest, and every three is 5 points harder (0.11 instead of 0.16 base). Shooters win 48.6%, handlers 52.9%, dunkers 49.4%, max strength 48.9% - all four within 4.3 points (it was 17). The shooters still make 66% of their shots (step-backs 68%, plain threes 50%) and score about 28 a game.

## How the players are made

Like the arcade originals, players are flat sprites (digitized frames) with a big head pasted on top.

- **Bodies**: `tools/gen.html` poses the realistic athlete body from `../hoops` through every move (run, dribble, jump shot, dunk, pass, steal, shove, fall, celebrate) in three facings and saves sprite sheets to `sprites/<id>.png` + `<id>.json` (frame anchors for the feet, head and hands).
- **Extras** (`sprites/<id>_x.png/.json`, a second sheet per player): the power-up (`powerup`, straight at the camera), the pumped-up `idle_buff`, `run_buff`, `drun_buff` and `dribble_buff`, and the taunts (`*_point`, `*_chopU`, `*_chopD`). The muscle pump scales bones in the skinned body while it renders (`BUFF` in gen.html).
- **Gold hair**: `faces/<id>_ssj.png` and `_ssj_back.png` are the photo heads with the hair turned gold and spiked up; `HW.SSJ_PAD` in `js/data.js` says where the photo sits in the bigger canvas. Carl's on-fire head is a different photo, his scowl, lined up on his normal head (same size and place), so he wears that face for as long as he's on fire; `HW.MOUTH_SSJ` puts the free-throw-line dunk's tongue on its mouth. His slow-motion dunks (under the legs, off the backboard) get yet another photo, his yell (`HW.DUNK_FACE`, `faces/carl_dunk.png`, and `carl_dunk_ssj.png` with the gold hair when he's on fire), from the moment the slow motion starts until he lands (`p.dunkFace`, sent to online guests with the tongue flag). The faces carry the sprites' version tag (`SHEET_V`).
- **Heads**: every player's face is cut out of a real photo (`faces/<id>_photo.png`, used for players with `photo: true` in `js/data.js`); the back of the head (`faces/<id>_back.png`) is rendered from the 3D head. A player without `photo: true` gets a rendered `faces/<id>_front.png` instead.

Rebuild the sprites after changing a player's look in `js/data.js` (`HW.PLAYERS` and `LOOK`; the athlete body itself comes from `../hoops/js/`). Needs Playwright:

```bash
node famjam/tools/gen.js
```

## Online (the family on their own phones)

Tap **ONLINE** on the title screen.

1. **Host:** one person taps HOST A GAME and gets a 4-digit room code. SEND INVITE opens the phone's share sheet with a link (`?room=1234`); on a computer the button copies the link instead.
2. **Join:** everyone else taps the link, or taps JOIN A GAME and types the code.
3. **Pick players:** in the lobby each person taps who they want to be. That's up to 4 people with 2 on a team; a player someone has already taken can't be picked.
4. **Together or against:** the host also sets the mode. **TOGETHER** puts up to 2 people on one team against the CPU, picking from any family; the CPU plays a family neither of them is in, and a team with two families in it plays as the **Family** (`setup.names`, `G.team()`). **AGAINST** puts people on opposite teams: at most 2 per family and two families at a time (someone alone against the CPU gets another family picked for them). The lobby has a column per family.
5. **Start:** the host picks the CPU difficulty and quarter length and taps START. Spots nobody picked are played by the CPU.

How it works (`js/net.js`):
- **The host runs the game.** Its phone runs the real game for everyone. The other phones only send their stick and buttons. The host sends back the picture about 60 times a second (every player, the ball, the scoreboard and the callouts, about 0.6 KB each), plus every sound and effect the game makes.
- **Guests draw it smoothly.** Each guest draws the host's game 0.08 s in the past and blends between updates, so it doesn't stutter.
- **Fair shooting.** A guest's jump-shot release is judged by where their own screen showed the jump when they let go (`p.relSt`), so lag doesn't spoil their timing.
- **Connections.** Phones connect straight to each other over WebRTC. PeerJS (`js/lib/peerjs.min.js`, MIT, loaded only when you go online) uses its free cloud server to introduce them, and its TURN servers relay when a network blocks a direct line.
- **Menus don't stop the game.** Opening the menu (II) on any phone doesn't pause it for everyone.
- **Someone drops out.** If a guest's phone drops, the CPU takes over their player. Tapping TRY AGAIN puts them back as the same player.
- **The host leaves.** If the host leaves, the game ends for everyone.
- **The host has a small edge.** The host plays with no lag at all, so the person with the best connection should host.
- **Testing.** For testing, `?peer=127.0.0.1:9000` points the game at your own PeerServer (npm `peer`) instead of the cloud one.

**Voice chat** (`js/voice.js`): talk while you play, on phones, the Quest (the VR page runs this same code) and computers - any browser that can use a microphone over WebRTC. VOICE in the lobby (bottom, beside LEAVE) or the pause menu turns it on; it asks for the microphone, and MUTE turns yours off while you keep listening. No microphone, or it isn't allowed: you still hear everyone (LISTENING ONLY). It rides the game's own PeerJS connections in a star: each guest's phone calls the host's with its mic (a PeerJS media call, `peer.call`), and the host's phone answers each one with everyone else's voices mixed together - its own mic and the other guests', never your own back to you (WebAudio, one mix per guest, `rewire`) - so nobody has to call everybody, and the host passes the voices on even with its own VOICE off. Echo cancelling, noise suppression and auto gain are on; the game's own sound drops to about half while it's on (`DUCK`), and headphones help. The lobby shows a mic by each person on voice (crossed out: listening only) and lights whoever's talking green; in the game a mic bobs over the head of whoever's talking (the host hears everyone, so it sends the list round, `vt`). Leaving the room turns it all off and lets go of the mic. On the Quest, tap MIC FOR VOICE CHAT on the VR page before ENTER VR so the headset asks about the microphone outside VR. Tested in headless browsers with three phones whose "mics" were different tones: each heard exactly the other two.

**Buzz a phone: invites with no texting** (`N.buzz` in `js/notify.js`, the BUZZ A PHONE and GET BUZZED screens in ui.js): from the Quest, a computer or a phone, ONLINE > BUZZ A PHONE (or OR BUZZ A PHONE in your room's lobby) shows the whole family: tap someone and you get a room (or use the one you're hosting) and their phone gets a notification - "FAM JAM: Tyler is calling you - Tyler wants to play FAM JAM with you! Tap to join room 1234." - and tapping it opens the game straight into the room (`?room=1234`). It works even when they don't have the game open, through ntfy, like the owner's notifications: each person sets it up once on their own phone with ONLINE > GET BUZZED - get the free ntfy app, then OPEN IN THE NTFY APP (Android) or type their own topic into it (`famjam-an8b5m62sbps-<their id>`, shown there in lower case; COPY IT copies it; OR THE NTFY WEBSITE subscribes in the browser) - and SEND ME A TEST checks it. A buzz only reaches phones that did that. Someone you just buzzed shows SENT for 30 s. The in-game CHALLENGE (above) still reaches anyone who has the game open, the Quest included.

**Who's online** (`js/presence.js`): the title screen shows how many people have FAM JAM open right now (N ONLINE NOW, under the ONLINE button; the online screen too). There's no server of our own: on PeerJS's cloud server the first phone on takes a well-known lobby ID (`famjam-lobby-v1`) and keeps the count, everyone else connects to it and checks in every 2 s, and it sends the count back out. A phone put in the background lets go; if the lobby's phone goes away the others race to take the ID over (about 2-3 s when it lets go, about 10 s when it just drops off). Nothing goes over the wire but a random ID, the count, who's who (below) and invites.

In an online game, as a guest in someone's room, or hosting with people already in, invites are ignored; and the "came on" pop-up closes itself if the newcomer comes straight into your room with the link. If two people invite each other at the same moment, the room with the lower code wins: its invite pops up for the other host, and the other room's invite is dropped.

**Phone notifications for the owner** (`js/notify.js`): when someone opens FAM JAM, the owner's phone gets a push notification through ntfy (`ntfy.sh`, a free push service, no sign-up; the ntfy app on the phone subscribes to the topic `famjam-an8b5m62sbps`): "Lisa just opened FAM JAM on a phone - 2 online now. Tap to play.", and tapping it opens the game: the family member they picked on WHO ARE YOU? (it waits up to 2 minutes for the pick, `PICK`; "Someone" if there's still none), and nothing else about them. A device sends at most one every 30 minutes (reloads don't buzz again), and the owner's own phones and computers never do: open the game once on each with `?me` (https://tanderson5620.github.io/Donut-Town/famjam/?me; the title screen says GOT IT: THIS DEVICE WON'T SEND YOU NOTIFICATIONS). The topic name is in this public code, so anyone who reads it could subscribe too. `?notify=127.0.0.1:port` posts to a local stand-in instead, and pages served from this machine don't post at all without it.

**Play counter** (`js/count.js`): an anonymous count of the devices that have played and the games started (a device once, kept with a flag in its own storage; a game once, on the phone running it - online, the host's), kept by Abacus (`abacus.jasoncameron.dev`, a free public counting service, no sign-up) under `tanderson5620-famjam` (`players`, `games`; read them at `https://abacus.jasoncameron.dev/get/tanderson5620-famjam/games`). The totals show at the bottom of the title screen. On the owner's own devices (marked with `?me`) the totals sit bottom left with a second line under them: N BY YOU, M BY OTHERS (+16 FROM BEFORE). By you is `owner-games`, which only his devices count (and they aren't added to `players`); by others is the rest since his count started; the 16 games played before that (`C.PRE`) were his and others' and can't be split. No names or anything about the player. `?count=127.0.0.1:port` counts on a local stand-in instead, and `?nostats` leaves the counter and who's online off (for testing).
